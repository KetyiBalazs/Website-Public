# **The designer, who can't be killed.**

Life has become quite different, since I moved to San Francisco (especially as a designer). The AI race is extremely prominent there - probably the most prominent in the entire world. Every week, something “kills” design.

A new AI model drops, someone releases a new design tool, a video on X demonstrates a $10,000 website “built” in minutes from a single prompt. Almost without exception, the people demonstrating these scenarios are the same ones selling the antidote. People should realise that these posts are mostly sales funnels with a dramatic opening.

I teach and mentor designers and leaders - I see that the fear is genuine, regardless of title and position. One observation that comes up almost universally is that the influence of the new tools are so extreme that people cannot keep up, thus they lose all clarity on what they are even supposed to implement.

## **The only skill that matters**

Designers keep asking me which skill to acquire next: whether to learn to code, which AI tools to master, which platform to bet on - should it be Claude or Cursor, Figma? What about MCPs and generating designs?

## **The only skill you really need is: Adaptation**

Spoiler Alert: Jujutsu Kaisen is one of the most popular manga and shows of recent years - and rightfully so. There are many religious and spiritual references carefully woven into the fabric of the story. There is a very specific part of the manga that features a creature called Mahoraga: a towering, muscular humanoid creature with an eight-handled wheel floating above its head. Arguably one of the strongest entities in the show, capable of challenging even the most powerful characters.

You might ask - what makes it so dangerous and strong?

![Mahoraga](/mahoraga.png)

## **It has but one skill: Adaptation. It can adapt to everything.**

Whenever exposed to an attack or technique, the wheel turns, allowing the creature to gradually adapt and become resistant or immune to that outside force. It has the capacity for near-limitless evolution. I think that is a beautiful way to demonstrate that you don’t need anything fancy to survive - you just have to be able to change and adapt to outside forces when needed. The reason I can type these lines while flying from Tokyo to San Francisco is because our species just kept adapting and adapting. Adapted to cold, heat, depths and heights. We are the result of almost limitless rounds of adaptation. When it comes to AI, it is the same.

When desktop publishing arrived in the 1980s, book designers feared that laser printers would flood the world with aesthetically mediocre documents - that it would drive professionals into unemployment and quality design into oblivion, since speed would be valued over craftsmanship.

They were describing the same panic that fills design Twitter today.

What actually happened is more instructive than the fear: the inflexible copyists joined the unemployed, while the adaptable ones became book designers for the very printers they had feared. The role did not disappear - it transformed. The broader cultural effect ran opposite to the prediction: design became democratised, standards for everyday documents rose, and awareness of what good design even looked like expanded across the entire population. The technology that was supposed to kill design - ended up making design everyone’s business.

A career built around mastering any specific tool has a shelf life measured in months - especially nowadays. A career built around the capacity to reorient, integrate, and move forward has no expiration date, because it is not indexed to any particular wave. It is indexed to YOU.

## **The misunderstanding underneath the panic**

What leadership tends to believe, and what some designers have unfortunately internalised, is that a designer is someone who executes on briefs and produces visual representations of decisions made elsewhere. On that definition, yes, our industry is in trouble. A sufficiently prompted model can produce Figma-ready interfaces faster than any human, and it will only get faster and better.

## **But that was never design in the first place. That was drafting.**

Design, properly understood, is applied judgment under uncertainty: deciding what problem is actually worth solving, for whom, in what form, and why.

As Paul Saffo put it: “It used to be that designers made an object and walked away; today the emphasis must shift to designing the entire life cycle.”

AI is collapsing the cost of visual execution to near zero, which means execution will stop being the scarce thing. Judgment, taste, and problem definition will become scarcer, not less so. The designers who have always confused their tools for their thinking are the ones in genuine danger. The ones who understood the distinction were never really threatened.

## **Do not forget - we only observe a slice of time and space**

What is important to recognise is that the industry was always changing. We changed the tools, refined the processes, digitalised the steps, shortened the delivery cycles. This is just another step - a much bigger and more influential one, but another step nonetheless. Before you fall into despair, realise that this is normal. Yes, there are many vultures online trying to capitalise on this moment and amplify the messaging beyond all reason. It frustrates me too, but it is what it is.

We have roles in companies for practical reasons: they make it easier to separate skills, define jobs, and hire specialists instead of generalists. That is useful. But it tricks people into thinking roles are permanent fixtures of the universe. They are not. Roles merge, split, and disappear all the time - not because of AI specifically, but because everything changes constantly. That is just how progress works.

We experience our careers in a very narrow vertical slice of time. A designer today might remember what the industry looked like five or ten years ago and squint three years into the future. But zoom out the way biology does, across millions of years of adaptation, and the mindset becomes obvious: the thing that survives is never the most specialised. It is the most adaptable. I remember when I started in design, it was the 3Ps: Pen, Paper, Photoshop - then it became Illustrator, InVision, Sketch, Figma. The tools cycle, and my job changed too. I started as a junior, became a senior - from employee to founder and studio owner, from mentee to teacher and mentor. I am a designer not because I can use Figma, but because of my thinking - and in recent years Figma was the most efficient tool to express and bring my ideas into the world. It may be different in the future.

## **AI is like fire**

People keep using the word “survive” because deep down many of them believe the role of a “designer” is about to go extinct. They see us being pushed more and more into engineering - and a designer who has to code isn’t a designer anymore. Or is it?

Fire is not a monster. It has no opinion about whether it heats your home or burns it down. The same is true of AI. The question was never whether the tool is good or bad; it is whether you are using it to solve the right problem for the right people in the right way. What AI does change - is the standards. If anyone can produce a visually polished website in ten minutes from a prompt (which we can’t) - then visual polish is worth roughly nothing. The value migrates, as it always does, to whatever is hardest to replicate. In the future, iteration will become faster than long design cycles. Teams will be smaller, people will have broader knowledge, and we will all become orchestrators rather than purely professionals who execute.

## **What I actually do**

There is enormous pressure on designers to keep up with the speed of engineers. Leadership gets frustrated when design falls behind, but what makes this hard is that design is rarely deterministic. The same speed increase that works for engineering cannot be replicated in design without compromise. Even for me it was very hard to adopt and integrate AI into most, if not all of my workflows. But I do not focus purely on speed, because it is not that simple.

You should sit down and map out how you work, and how that workflow connects to other workflows in the organisation. Then list the problems you are trying to solve and identify the parts that can be automated, or where a targeted AI integration can genuinely help your process - whether that means speed, quality, decision-making, or exploration. Then integrate, test, and iterate. I personally do not believe in one-shot prompting. It produces a quick end result, which is useful for iteration, but it carries significant downsides.

**Some examples of what I do:**

- Better and wider exploration (with Mobbin MCP)
- Generate placeholder animations from Figma frames (with Jitter)
- Turn ideas or meeting notes into mind maps and user flows with FigJam & Claude
- Turn meeting notes into design ideas (with Internode)
- Turn flowcharts into editable wireframes with the Figma Agent
- Turn Figma tokens (JSON) into clean code with AI
- Turn designs into code with Cursor
- Write documentation and extra context for developers
- Design critique where AI surfaces things you should be aware of
- Accessibility checks with AI
- Bug detection (pre-written code and automation for your website)

In the future, humans will be governors and not purely executors. That is the future you have to prepare for, and the time is now. Designers have been confined to the role of executor for too long. This technological shift will push some designers to step up, to step outside the safe auto-layout-pushing framework of Figma, to learn more disciplines, and to take on leadership roles. This is just a snapshot of how I see the world and the industry today. Tomorrow, it might change.
